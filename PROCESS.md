# How this manual was made: a process record

Chih Chen, November 2026

This is the working record behind "Cash That Never Sleeps". It sets out who did what, stage by stage and draft by draft, what went wrong, who or what caught it, and what changed as a result. I have tried to be as hard on the models I relied on most as on the ones I used less, and as hard on myself as on any of them. Every row in the error log rests on a dated file in the project record: a brief, a review memo, a model output, a commit, a build log or the outside versions themselves.

The manual is the fifth stage of one experiment. The four stages before it produced working drafts that never left my desk: none was posted, released or circulated, and the repository that held them was private. They are described here as stages and drafts of this experiment, by stage number and draft number, and are not cited anywhere as publications. Their results live in the manual and in the engines under `model/`.

## 1. The experiment

One real problem sits under all seven stages. A regional bank holds commercial cash it cannot insure, and that cash runs on two clocks the bank does not run on. The run clock: uninsured commercial cash can leave in hours, as it did in March 2023. The settlement clock: a tokenized or always-on deposit can move at any hour on any day, while the bank's large-value settlement, its repo market, the discount window and its asset sales keep business hours. FedNow runs at all hours, but for payments of limited size; the mismatch sits on the funding and liquidity side.

The experiment asks whether frontier AI models, working under my direction, can do the financial engineering on a problem like this: find the facts and check them against primary sources, build a reproducible model, design answers for which no verified product exists, compare those answers honestly under a rule set before any result, and write it up in a form that survives a regulator or a rating analyst. It also asks where they still need a human. The models did the engineering. I set the problems, made the decisions and decided what was verified enough to stand behind. The last step is review by human experts, which the manual's closing ask requests and which has not yet happened.

The work ran in seven stages:

## 3. Stage 1: the clearing cost and the pooled vehicle

Dates 17 October to 1 November 2026. Draft numbers are the stage-1 draft's own sequence; versions 4 and 9 were produced outside the main workflow and are marked as such.

### What happened

On 17 October 2026 I had an AI-drafted white paper on private protection for uninsured bank deposits. GPT Astra (OpenAI) wrote it and Gemini Argon (Google) enhanced it. The idea was mine: a large uninsured corporate balance carries a price, and if you could compute that price you could read it as the clearing cost of holding the balance uninsured, whether or not anyone pays it. I asked for a red-team review and a version I could post.

The review said the idea held up and the draft could not go out. Over the next day the draft was rebuilt from a verified-facts base, and partway through I changed the question. From round 3 (18 October) the work was also a test: can frontier AI models financially engineer a credible answer to a real problem for which no verified product exists, with empirical facts that check out and a design explicit, ranged and stress-tested enough to survive a regulator or a rating analyst? And where do they still need a human? That question is the experiment this record follows through all seven stages.

### What was built

One Python engine that produces every stage-1 number and figure, now `model/clearing_cost.py` with results in `model/results_clearing_cost.md`. It prices a large uninsured balance in four terms (credit, access, capacity, frictions) under one pricing measure, with no stacked premiums; prices a single-name design and a pooled 50-bank vehicle; computes reliability given the depositor's own bank fails under a Gaussian and a Student-t(5) copula; sets the three-party clearing condition; runs Year 2 after a loss year; and checks the four-term formula against full discounting (within 0.016 basis points). The reference design, an all-funded 20 percent buffer across 50 banks, priced at 37.7 basis points. The draft grew from about 6,400 to about 12,400 words of body text with 11 figures and 14 tables, and ran to 54 pages at draft 31.

### Checks run

## 4. Stage 2: the mutual with a tail

Date 1 November 2026. The stage ran in one day, from a design session to a gated draft, followed in the evening by a second fact check aimed at the premise.

### What it was for

Section 6 of the stage-1 draft told a holder of passive corporate cash that it is paid for the risk and should not buy protection. Read by a depositor, that says move the money to a government fund or a money-center bank, and the 2023 record fits that in part: the large balances that ran left the banking system to a considerable degree, and the regionals that ran replaced them with insured, reciprocal and brokered money that costs more than the core deposits it stands in for. (The first version of the stage-2 draft said the uninsured corporate money left and did not come back; the version 7 fact check, below, corrected that.) I asked whether regional banks acting together could do better than watch it go. The design came out of a structured interview in four rounds, each a set of decisions I made, and a build-and-review loop on the numbers.

## 5. Stage 3: the always-on problem

Dates 31 October to 2 November 2026. The stage kept a build log, not a numbered error log; the stage-3 rows in Section 8 are built from that log and the draft's internal notes.

### What it was for

The stage-1 draft had covered a bank that fails. In stage 1 I had asked whether it covered cash access at a bank that is closed but has not failed (D1 in the log), and the models had not. Stage 3 took that up as the settlement clock: a tokenized deposit moves at any hour, while Fedwire Funds runs 22 hours on weekdays, the discount window, Fedwire Securities, the repo market and asset sales keep business hours, and FedNow runs at all hours but for payments of limited size. The gap is about 50 hours each weekend plus holidays. In the same design session I added a second question in my own words: how private protection compares with the secured routes a bank already has (securities-collateralized deposits, Home Loan Bank letters of credit, other lines), on price and on how much of the deposit the bank can still lend.

## 6. Stage 4: the eight-route comparison

### What it was for

## 9. What only I caught or decided

Only three entries in the log were caught by me alone: D1 and D2 in stage 1, and D3 after stage 7. That understates the human part, because most of what I contributed was decisions, not catches. The models surfaced most of the problems; somebody still had to decide what each draft was, what it would claim, and what was verified enough to stand behind.

Stage 1:

- The problem and the thesis. The clearing-cost reading of the price, and the point that the price matters even if nobody pays it, were mine from the start.
- The purpose. Turning the work into a test of AI financial engineering, with verified-only facts and a design built to be attacked, was my round-3 decision.
- Posture. Generic naming, no "stigma" language, "protection" not "insurance", the views-my-own and disclosure lines. The models proposed these rules in the first review; the outside versions broke them twice; holding them was my call each time.
- What to reject. I rejected most of version 4 and much of version 9, kept their good ideas, and required every outside idea to be rebuilt in our own model before it could stay.
- Owning parameters. I refused to have the 20 percent capacity presented as my judgment, set the frictions floor at 10 basis points instead of 7.5, and accepted the 45 basis point buffer premium only as a sweep point.
- Design conventions. The conventions the pricing rests on were proposed by the models and reviewed and approved by me: the four-term decomposition and the rule of one pricing measure with no stacked premiums (written into the first brief after the first review, A2), wrong-way risk as a haircut to the protection's value, and the simultaneous timing standard (the modelling model's convention from model v5).
- The final design. I made the all-funded buffer the reference design, put reliability given the depositor's own bank fails in the abstract under both copulas, and aligned replenishment to pre-funding.
- Facts. I decided the rate update and which version of the Correia, Luck and Verner study to cite, after the primary sources were fetched.
- Disclosure. I decided to name the models and to keep this record with the manual.
- Commissioning outside challenge. The two outside versions were my choice. Without them the stage-1 draft would still cite superseded guidance, still exclude correct FDIC figures, and still lead with a dearer design.
- The question the models did not ask (D1), and how an operating account is actually paid (D2).

Stage 2. The design itself, at every branch: that the client is regional banks together and not one bank; that fund-equivalent safety under 25 basis points is the bar; that reciprocal placement may be a layer even though it leans on the Deposit Insurance Fund; that the mutual is a private licensed insurer and not a fund by statute; that surviving members carry capped special assessments; that the tail is sized to the Gaussian standard with the fat-tailed result disclosed rather than to the fat-tailed standard; that the Massachusetts settlement rule is primary. Also the reading of the credit evidence: an availability claim at community and smaller regional banks, not a pricing claim. And the questions on reciprocal networks that led to N15 to N19.

Stage 3. Taking up D1 as a problem in its own right; the comparison of private protection with the secured routes a bank already has, asked in my own words partway through the design session; keeping all seven extensions, each with its pros, cons and what breaks.

Stage 4. The two problems, the eight routes, the five screens and the rule, fixed before any result; that an open verdict is not a pass; one comparison on top, with the earlier drafts kept as working stages; keeping the closing ask for challenge against the style grader (U39).

Stage 5. One manual in place of four drafts; that none of the earlier drafts is cited as a publication; that the always-on material goes public inside the manual; that where a source draft and an engine disagree, the engine wins; the ruling on F14.

Stage 6. That the published answer to the settlement clock was not yet an answer; the four additions and a separate fifth engine; the settlement clock's own headline; the lendable-share convention (V2); the page budget and the cuts.

Stage 7. Commissioning an outside review from another model family before the human one; reframing the settlement simulation as each member's peak net debit in an open debtor cohort rather than building a payment-path model, with the median the pilot must observe left as the stated input; that the tail advances the assessment at closure under rule M, at the price that follows; that the Sunday cash source is specified and the prefunded joint-account arrangement priced as a ninth route and run through the same rule as the other eight; F05 and F07 fixed in the text and sent to counsel, not modelled; the drafter's cut list at about 86 pages. After the reviews, the charge by kind of deposit (D3), and a second interactive page for the settlement clock.

## 10. What the models did well, and badly

### Well

- Speed. From the first model run to a gated, audited draft 12 took about 18 hours, including two outside versions. Stage 2 ran from a design session to a gated draft in one day; stage 4 from plan to a 46-page PDF in under four hours.
- Breadth. The first review covered empirical sources, liquidity and capital rules, receivership law, UCC assignment, setoff, deposit-broker rules and securities law in one pass, and its legal findings held up through every later round.
- Reproducible engines. Every number in the manual comes from five scripts under `model/`. The stage-1 audit reran its engine in an empty folder and got byte-identical results and figures; the stage-5 code move held numeric parity on every one of 11,181 numbers across the four results files.
- Number tracing. The gate on stage-1 draft 10 traced about 1,450 numbers and found eight errors (C15); the audit then traced 1,799 numeric tokens in the corrected draft and found none contradicted by its source. The stage-2 and stage-5 fact checks matched every table cell to its results file, and the stage-4 check found no model number wrong. That is a level of checking I would not have done by hand in a day.
- Flagging instead of fixing silently. The modelling model raised 12 specification issues at stage-1 model v1 for me to rule on, rather than quietly changing the brief; in stage 2 it reported that the design as briefed missed the bar (N1 to N3) rather than tuning it to pass; in stage 4 it added the deciding legal question (U2).
- Cross-model challenge worked. Each family found things the others missed. In stage 7 GPT Astra reproduced every number it recomputed, one extreme tail percentile only within simulation error, and still found four definitions and one cash-timing assumption wrong, none of which the Claude checks of the earlier stages had flagged. Gemini Argon, reviewing the next version, reran every engine byte for byte and found that three claims went further than the engines did; it was also wrong on two findings, and its numbers for a third were off, which the fresh Claude checks caught before any change. The most consequential point of that round was one neither outside review stated: a route the manual had set aside on a screen its own rule does not contain. The original GPT Astra and Gemini Argon draft supplied the framing that survived every stage: two risks in one balance (the eventual credit loss and the loss of access), the gap between the cash needed at failure and the eventual loss, the concentration of the 2023 runs in the top 0.5 percent of depositors, and the point that unfunded guarantees fail together with the banks they cover. Gemini contributed the passive-versus-operating cash split and the pooled vehicle. Version 9 rebuilt our pooled model faithfully (its Gaussian tables match ours to the third decimal) and contributed four ideas we adopted.
- A rule that bit. The stage-4 rule, run by a model, named a strict champion I would not have picked, and the draft reported it (U2).

### Badly

- Confident errors. A 99 percent reliability headline that held under one copula only; "prove" language for illustrative results; a buffer that "covers six failures" when it covers five at the moment of payment.
- Invented parameters stated as fact. A 1.67 basis point repo fee, a 45 basis point buffer premium, 7.5 basis points of frictions, a 35 to 60 basis point investor hurdle, and a rate deck attributed to a paper that reports no prices.
- Setting targets so the design passes. A 1.01 percent reliability standard equal to the design's own result, in a write-up whose own code used 1.05.
- Dropping the inconvenient result. Version 9's code computed that the fat-tailed standard needs about 48 percent capacity, and its write-up presented 20 percent as enough (A20).
- Citation drift. A study mis-dated for eight versions and then briefly swapped for an older preprint; superseded guidance cited as current; an FHFA rule cited for bank supervision; a swaps provision cited for security-based swaps; a NY Fed paper cited for prices it does not report; a Federal Register page carried wrong through five drafts (W14).
- Stale facts. A risk-free rate four tenths of a point off, model-risk guidance six months out of date, and a reciprocal-deposit allowance replaced before the stage-1 draft was even written, which no Claude check flagged through thirty drafts (A21). The stale rate was caught only when the pipeline checked FRED directly.
- Over-caution, the mirror image. The Claude pipeline barred correct figures it could not verify from search results, while a copy of the primary source sat in my library. Being conservative about facts is right; being conservative without opening the primary source cost three rounds.
- Losing posture and disclaimers. Both outside versions dropped the views-my-own line and the first-person disclosure line (version 9 kept a third-person note on its use of AI), and version 4 brought back the named-bank benchmark.
- Attribution drift. A parameter chosen by a model written up as my judgment; a methods note that overstated how much I had personally checked against primary sources; a question I asked written up as a reader's (W8). Left alone, the models would have made me look both more responsible for their choices and more thorough than the record shows.
- Overconfident review. The Claude review of version 9 said its workpaper did not exist and called two of its results not reproducible, without asking for the script; when I obtained and ran it, both results reproduced and one diagnosis turned out wrong (C22 to C24).
- Same-family blind spots. The stage-1 gate and audit were Claude reviewing Claude. They found real errors at every round (29, 31, 11 and 10 required fixes, then 4 defects), but none of them caught the superseded guidance, the correct figures on the barred list, or the cheaper design already sitting in our own table. The outside versions did. Stages 2 to 6 had no outside family at all, and the stage-7 review found what their checks had missed.
- A fabricated citation. The version 23 round cited two authors under a Federal Reserve working-paper number that belongs to a different paper, for a 2023 finding their real paper never makes (E1). It reached a built PDF before the fact check caught it.
- Carrying a number across builds. A sentence written on stage-2 build 1 survived into prose on build 2 (N4); a stage-2 comparison survived into the manual against a different benchmark (F4).
- Style fixes that break facts. Sentence splits moved a citation, a figure swap left its caption reversed, an added "so" turned a description into a false cause (U49 to U53); round-1 recasts in stage 3 left three sentences that did not parse (W12).

## 11. What remains unverified and needs domain experts

Stage 1, the clearing cost and the vehicle:

- Payments and receivership plumbing. Whether a payment triggered by the FDIC's appointment as receiver can in practice be made at par within the promised business days; how the vehicle would collect advance dividends and later dividends as assignee of the deposit claim; how the receivership cutoff, setoff and the receiver's repudiation powers interact with an assignment; the queued-wire Thursday and the closed-but-not-failed gap (D1).
- Legal wrappers. The standby letter of credit route, the security-based swap route and the dealer-registration threshold, state financial guaranty insurance licensing, the deposit-broker facilitation question, and the insured-status labelling rules. All are stated as a practitioner's reading, not legal advice, and need counsel.
- Bank capital and liquidity treatment of a bank-provided facility. The 100 percent conversion factor, my reading of the liquidity rule, and the resulting cost floor of 55.4 to 117.0 basis points need a regulatory specialist.
- Tail calibration. The correlation is illustrative, not calibrated. The copula choice moves conditional reliability from about 1 percent to about 28 percent at the same capacity, and the capacity needed for a 1 percent standard from 22 to 68 percent. Default probability, the risk-premium multiplier, deposit loss, the advance dividend and the time to final dividends are illustrative. State-dependent loss is a modelling assumption.
- Market prices. The buffer lock-up premium, any facility fee and the 15 basis points of frictions have no observed market price. There is no evidenced repo market for receivership certificates.
- Behaviour. Whether protection arranged in advance changes how a depositor behaves in a run is an open empirical question; stage 1 proposed a pilot.
- Facts resting on secondary reports. The USDC price path, the tokenized Treasury market size, the Maryland 1985 dates, the Massachusetts fund and the discount window disclosure lag were confirmed through reputable reports, not the primary source. Three correct FDIC figures (B7) still need page references.

Stage 2, the mutual:

- The acquirer probability at closure (0.5 as an author assumption) and its behaviour in a cluster; the risk-class mix under state-dependent loss given default (not run within the budget); the insurance-law route for the mutual (admitted mutual insurer or risk retention group; financial guaranty classification), which needs counsel; whether a Texas bank's pledge under 34.304(c) reaches a capital contribution; the Massachusetts fund's risk classes and rates; the 2023 deposit-to-credit link at regional banks, which no study has measured.
- Whether the uninsured money the surviving regionals hold today belongs to the depositors who ran (the data carry no depositor type); the share of what ran that was passive corporate cash; whether a second published bank cap exists beyond the one cited; and whether the stage-1 remedy terms, which freeze new cover on a covenant breach, carry over to the mutual, which would make its cover stop growing for a weakening member as the base layer does.

Stage 3, the always-on problem:

- The QFC treatment of a weekend repo when a member is closed on a Friday: creditors wait until 5 p.m. ET on the next business day at the earliest, so the facility covers loss, not Monday-morning liquidity. Needs counsel.
- State-bank rules on pledging assets for private deposits were not researched. The California collateral percentages were read from a secondary reproduction of the statute because the official site blocked the fetch.
- The haircut (2 percent over three days), the cap basis and the always-on outflow distribution are illustrative. The reciprocal placement fee rests on one bank's disclosure, not a network rate. The US status of an omnibus-account model and some 24-hour transfer claims for tokenized funds rest on secondary reports.

Stage 4, the comparison:

- Whether a national bank may make the 3 percent entry capital contribution to a state-licensed excess-deposit mutual insurer (the mutual's screen S1). This decides the strict champion. For counsel.
- Whether a private corporate depositor at a national bank may be the beneficiary of a Home Loan Bank letter of credit (the letter's screen S1).
- The protected weekend repo's priority over the receiver (the facility's screen S4), bounded at 1.38 basis points a year under the real-world measure and 4.15 under the pricing measure.
- Every engine parameter not tied to a cited source is an author assumption, labelled in the results file; the always-on outflow distribution and the shared-infrastructure saving are illustrative.
- Stage-4 draft 4 carries four in-place fixes that no fresh instance graded, by the loop's cap.
- The run-time and byte-identity claims rest on the results-file header (two runs identical) and the modelling model's memo (four runs identical, about 83 seconds of import time).

Stage 5, the manual (open):

- The weekday hours of the FedNow liquidity management transfer, to be read from the Federal Reserve's schedule page directly (F7).
- The Federal Register page of the reciprocal-deposit rule was not reached in the stage-5 check; Fedwire Securities hours still carry no reference.
- 24 items were unverifiable in the stage-5 fact check.
- Review by human experts, the last step of the experiment, has not yet happened.

Stage 6, the settlement clock:

- The weekend outflow. The cap ladder turns on the median weekend outflow of tokenized deposits and its dispersion, both assumed. No bank has reported them, and the finding that a lendable balance and a rare cap cannot both hold is conditional on them.
- Dependence and scaling. The factor correlations, the equal member sizes, independent weekends and the linear scaling of outflow with closed hours are illustrative; the receiving side is not modelled.
- The buffer. Whether a bank's existing liquid buffer can also back the weekend facility, and how a supervisor would treat the double call on it, needs a liquidity specialist.
- Legal and operational plumbing. Sponsored access, the receivership stay applied to the facility's repos, re-use of collateral within tri-party repo, the effective date of the FICC collateral-in-lieu offering, and any use of FedNow liquidity management transfers outside business hours for this purpose need counsel and the operators.

Stage 7, the outside review:

- The assumed median peak net debit. The settlement results now rest on each member's peak net debit, with a median of 5 percent of the tokenized balance assumed, not observed. At a dispersion of 0.8 the lendable-screen verdict turns between medians of 1.5 and 2 percent. No public source reports participant-level peak debits on tokenized balances; a pilot would need timestamped attempted and completed payments, receivers and queued orders.
- Repeated weekend resets. Each weekend starts fresh and settles at the Sunday opening; repeated resets without replenishment, and serial dependence across weekends, are not modeled. Under seven-day operation the reserves-held sizing uses a 50-hour or 65.25-hour window as an illustrative bracket, not an estimate.
- Interest on joint-account balances outside RTP. R9's base assumes the balance earns the rate on reserve balances, which rests on a Board order specific to the RTP joint account. A new joint account would need its own determination; earning nothing, R9 prices at 103.35 and R8 returns as challenger. The liquidity coverage treatment of a participant's share of a joint-account balance has no public statement.
- Settlement on the custodian's books. Whether a closed member's bills could be sold or repoed on the tri-party custodian's own books over the weekend or on Monday, which would shorten the 35.5-hour operational interval, is not established by any source read. Needs the custodians and counsel.
- The tail's advance of the assessment carries no credit charge: a survivor that cannot pay within the week would leave the tail with an unpriced claim. The clearing-cost engine still prints a rounded run time in its results header (T4) and was not rerun in stage 7.

## 12. Lessons for using frontier models in financial engineering

Stage 1:

1. Use more than one model family and make them review one another. They fail in different directions. Here, Claude erred toward caution and stale regulation; GPT Astra and Gemini erred toward confident, unsourced parameters and posture regressions. Each caught errors the other could not see.
2. Make the primary source the arbiter, never another model, and check the library you already hold before trusting a search extract.
3. Keep a written facts sheet with an explicit status for each item (verified, secondary, barred) and reopen the barred items when a primary source becomes available. A do-not-reuse list is itself a set of claims that can be wrong.
4. Insist that every number comes from one reproducible workpaper, and rerun it in an empty folder before relying on it.
5. Set reliability standards and other targets before the run, in round numbers. A target read off the design's own result is not a standard.
6. Treat every parameter without an evidenced source as a sweep, never as a base case.
7. Date-stamp anything that moves (rates, guidance, thresholds) and recheck it on the day it goes out.
8. Write posture rules and disclaimers down and check them every round. Models regress on them.
9. Watch attribution in both directions. Models will credit you with their choices and credit themselves (or each other) vaguely. The methods note should say who chose what.
10. Same-family review is not independent review. An internal gate catches transcription, logic and consistency errors well; it shares its drafter's blind spots on facts and framing.
11. Ask what the problem left out. The models answered the problem they were given, thoroughly. The gap a treasurer would notice first came from a practitioner's question.

Stage 2:

12. A design brief states a cluster in members; a pool of 150 makes ten members a small share. State the exhaustion point as a reliability standard and let the model find the capacity.
13. Price decompositions are the finding. Most of the mutual's price is running cost; the design lives on frictions, so the benchmarks for frictions (two live excess insurers) mattered more than any copula.
14. A draft written on the second build inherited one sentence from the first. The fact check against the results file caught it; nothing else would have.
15. Gate fixes create tells of their own. The second grading round found a pattern the first round's fixes had made. Fresh graders each round, and a cap, are the protection.
16. Hedging a fact as unread is only honest while it is unread. When the primary text lands mid-draft, the hedge becomes an error.
17. A premise can pass a 501-check fact check when every number in it is right. The checks verified the cells; nobody had asked whether the cells carried the claim. Ask the inference question separately, and ask it of the sentence a reader will quote.

Stage 3:

18. A fact sheet built for one draft is a check on every draft that shares its facts. The stage-3 sheet found the stale reciprocal-deposit allowance in the stage-1 draft (A21), and the stage-1 correction found the stage-3 citation page wrong (W14).
19. A style grader reading closely catches facts too (W10, W11), and a style fixer breaks them (W12, W13). The fact check is not done when the style rounds start.
20. Draw the figure before writing the caption. A caption written to a specification described a chart that was never built (W15), and the chart that was built carried an older menu into the next stage (U15).

Stage 4:

21. A rule stated before the run can produce a champion the author would not have chosen, and an honest write-up reports it. The strict reading names the vehicle because one legal question about the mutual is open; the draft said so and made the question the ask.
22. Every style pass needs a scoped fact check behind it. Sentence splits moved a citation; a figure swap left its caption reversed; an added "so" turned a description into a false cause. Five of the five new slips in draft 2 came from edits that touched no number.
23. The engine's standard is stricter than the prose that quotes it. "About 1 percent" had covered 1.0084 percent through three drafts; sizing both designs to the same standard moved the vehicle's price by 0.20 basis points and the comparison onto level ground.
24. The fact checker who opens the image catches what the one who reads the caption cannot. Two of the stage-4 figure fails were visible only by looking.

Stage 5 (provisional):

25. Move code by number, not by byte. Extracting every number before and after the move, in order, proved parity on 11,181 numbers while allowing labels and paths to change; a byte test would have failed on the label changes alone.
26. A text can quote every engine number correctly and still be wrong about them. The stage-5 check found no misquoted number and three wrong statements built on correct numbers (F4, F5, F6): a difference taken against the wrong benchmark, a range read off two cells, a threshold never computed.
27. Parity in place is not reproducibility. The engines matched their own results in the working tree, and the documented command still failed in a clean clone (F2). Run the command a reader will run, from a fresh copy.
28. The records need checking too. Two stage records described drafts that never left my desk in words a reader would take as release (F14). A process record is a set of claims like any other.

Stage 6 (provisional):

29. A published answer is a claim to recheck. The settlement-clock answer had passed every gate of stage 5 and still did not answer the question it was built for; reading the result against the question, not against the checks, found it.
30. A sourcing pass's "not found" is a finding about the search, not about the world. The approval order a sourcing agent missed sat on the regulator's own listing; a second, independent reader found it.
31. Scan the code, not only the text. A residue gate on the PDF and on named strings missed internal folder names in a code comment that had been public for a stage.

Stage 7 (provisional):

32. Verified numbers can measure the wrong thing. Same-family checks in every stage since the second traced the mutual and settlement numbers to their engines; a reviewer from another family reproduced them and asked what they measured, and four definitions and one cash-timing assumption failed. Ask what each number is a measurement of, not only whether it matches its source.
33. Ask where the cash comes from on the day it is due. Two designs counted something as cash before it was: pledged bills at the Sunday opening (F01) and an assessment still receivable at closure (F04).
34. A dismissal is a claim to check. The drafts set prefunding aside because a pool seemed to need its own entity; a working instant-payment network runs on such a pool, and priced it became the settlement-clock challenger (T3).
35. Check the reviewer too, and check who gets the blame. One pass on primary sources found the review's single factual slip (T1); the fact check then found the draft attributing the manual's own error to the review (FC02). An outside review is a set of claims like any other.
